# KGB Arcade — Arcade-True Doctrine

**Status:** Non-negotiable production doctrine  
**Applies to:** Every KGB Arcade game, cartridge, mission, mode, remake, sequel, and gameplay-system decision.

KGB Arcade is not trying to recreate the visual limitations of old arcade cabinets. It is preserving the part that mattered: **games you can learn, master, fail at, immediately try again, and eventually beat because you got better.**

## The promise

A KGBA game should reward either exceptional natural skill or the stubborn willingness to play again and again until the player understands it.

Failure is allowed. Failure is often desirable. **Meaningless failure is not.**

A player should be able to lose, understand something new, try again, perform differently, and see improvement.

## The rules

### 1. Learnable beats arbitrary
The game may surprise the player, but it may not cheat the player. Core systems, hazards, timing windows, enemy behavior, routes, tells, and consequences must be consistent enough to learn.

### 2. Repeatable beats disposable
The same situation should generally obey the same rules. Repetition is not a flaw when repetition is how mastery is built.

### 3. Failure must teach
After a loss, the player should be able to answer at least one of these:
- What did I do wrong?
- What should I notice sooner next time?
- What pattern did I just learn?
- What input or decision should I change?

If the answer is only “the game randomly screwed me,” the design failed.

### 4. Randomness is bounded
Randomness can choose among known patterns, vary timing inside fair windows, change presentation, or keep repeated sessions fresh. It may not create unavoidable hits, unreadable situations, or outcomes that invalidate learning.

When determinism makes the game better, prefer determinism.

### 5. Skill and perseverance both count
A naturally gifted player may succeed quickly. Another player should be able to earn the same success by learning patterns, timing, routes, controls, and decisions over repeated attempts.

### 6. Readability beats spectacle
The player must be able to see what matters. Hazards, targets, objectives, timing cues, safe spaces, state changes, and feedback outrank visual flourish.

Effects that obscure play are defects, no matter how good they look.

### 7. Controls must become invisible
Inputs should be immediate, consistent, predictable, and easy to remember. Difficulty should come from the game—not from fighting the controls.

### 8. Fun beats looking nice
A crude game that makes people grin and hit Restart is closer to KGBA than a beautiful game that becomes boring after thirty seconds.

Visual polish supports gameplay. It does not substitute for gameplay.

### 9. Challenge escalates; rules do not mutate unfairly
Higher difficulty may demand faster recognition, tighter execution, longer endurance, more simultaneous threats, or deeper pattern knowledge. It should not secretly change fundamental rules simply to manufacture failure.

### 10. Restart is sacred
A failed run should flow naturally into another attempt. Avoid unnecessary menus, delays, cutscenes, confirmation dialogs, or friction between failure and replay.

The desired reaction is: **“Again.”**

### 11. Mastery should be visible
Scores, distance, time, rank, sector progress, clean streaks, boss phases, route completion, or another meaningful measure should let players recognize improvement.

### 12. The game must survive the no-art test
Ask: if the graphics were replaced with rectangles and circles tomorrow, would the core interaction still be fun?

If not, fix the game before polishing the art.

## Arcade-True and nontraditional KGBA games

Not every KGBA title needs lives, a fail screen, or a high score. TankTots, sandbox games, learning games, music toys, and experimental-input games can have different success models.

They still inherit the important parts of the doctrine:
- predictable response;
- learnable interaction;
- readable feedback;
- low friction;
- genuine fun;
- repeat play that becomes richer as the player understands the system.

## Final design test

Before shipping or substantially changing a game, ask:

> **Does playing again make the player better at this game—or merely give the game another chance to roll the dice?**

KGBA ships the first one.
